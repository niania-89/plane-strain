Strain Fundamentals

What is strain?

Simple idea: stress is the force per area a material feels; strain is how much it stretches or distorts as a fraction of its size. Pull a metal rod and it gets longer; twist a shaft and its surface lines tilt.

Strain has no units because it is length ÷ length (or an angle in radians). Small values are quoted in microstrain: $1\,\mu\varepsilon=10^{-6}$. Elastic deformation disappears when the load is removed; plastic (permanent) deformation does not.

Direct strain

$$\varepsilon=\frac{\Delta L}{L}$$ $\Delta L$ = change in length, $L$ = original length. Tension → positive, compression → negative.

Example: 250 mm bar shortens 0.16 mm: $\varepsilon=-0.16/250=-640\times10^{-6}$ (compressive).

Shear strain

Shear changes the angle between two lines that were perpendicular. Engineering shear strain $\gamma$ = the change in that angle (radians). Shear stress $\tau$ is a force effect; $\gamma$ is the resulting distortion, linked by $\tau=G\gamma$.

⚠ Tensor shear strain is $\varepsilon_{xy}=\gamma_{xy}/2$. This site always uses engineering $\gamma$; the Mohr circle plots $\gamma/2$.

Change in Length, Area & Volume

Length: $\Delta L=\varepsilon L$. Example: $\varepsilon=800\mu\varepsilon$, $L=2$ m → $\Delta L=1.6$ mm.

Area: a rectangle $a\times b$ becomes $a(1+\varepsilon_x)\times b(1+\varepsilon_y)$. Expanding: $\Delta A/A=\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y+\varepsilon_x\varepsilon_y\approx\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y$ (product is tiny, so ignored — an approximation).

Volume: $\varepsilon_v=\Delta V/V\approx\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y+\varepsilon_z$. In plane stress, $\sigma_z=0$ but $\varepsilon_z=-\frac{\nu}{E}(\sigma_x+\sigma_y)=-\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}(\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y)$.

Use Calculator 3 for numbers. Exam tip: state "small-strain approximation" when using the sums.

How to Draw Mohr's Circle for Strain – Step by Step

Stress vs strain circle: same method, but the vertical axis is γ/2 (tensor shear), not γ. Horizontal axis = ε. Example: $\varepsilon_x=400,\ \varepsilon_y=100,\ \gamma_{xy}=300$ (all ×10⁻⁶).

Exam tip: Axes → centre C → plot A(εx, +γ/2) and B(εy, −γ/2) → join (centre on the line) → draw circle. Angles on the circle are 2θ; physical angles are half.

Physical element vs circle

$\tan 2\theta_p=\gamma_{xy}/(\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y)=300/300=1\Rightarrow 2\theta_p=45^\circ,\ \theta_p=22.5^\circ$ (ccw from x for $\varepsilon_1$). Max-shear planes are 45° from principal planes (90° on the circle).

Strain Gauges

A bonded resistance strain gauge is a thin metal foil grid on a plastic backing, glued (adhesive) to the specimen, with leads soldered to the grid. When the part stretches, the grid stretches: it gets longer and thinner, so its resistance rises. It measures strain along the grid direction only. Used on aircraft wings, fuselage, landing gear, test rigs.

Installation steps

  1. Clean & degrease surface
  2. Mark location
  3. Align gauge with measuring direction
  4. Apply adhesive
  5. Press gauge on
  6. Cure
  7. Solder leads
  8. Connect to instrument (bridge)
  9. Apply load
  10. Record reading

Alignment matters: a gauge misaligned by angle $\phi$ reads $\varepsilon_\phi=\varepsilon_x\cos^2\phi+\varepsilon_y\sin^2\phi+\gamma_{xy}\sin\phi\cos\phi$, not the strain you wanted.

Measurement

$$GF=\frac{\Delta R/R}{\varepsilon}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varepsilon=\frac{\Delta R/R}{GF}$$ $GF$ = gauge factor (≈2 for foil), $R$ = original resistance, $\Delta R$ = change. A Wheatstone bridge turns the tiny $\Delta R$ into a measurable voltage. Quarter: 1 active gauge; half: 2; full: 4 (more output, temperature compensation).

Rosette strain at any angle

Rectangular rosette gauges at 0°, 45°, 90° read ε₁,ε₂,ε₃. Then $\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_1$, $\varepsilon_y=\varepsilon_3$, $\gamma_{xy}=2\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_3$ — feed these into the Mohr circle.

Applying Stress–Strain Equations

Young's modulus $E=\sigma/\varepsilon$ (slope of the straight part of the σ–ε graph; steel ≈ 200 GPa is stiff, rubber is flexible). Poisson's ratio $\nu=-\varepsilon_{lat}/\varepsilon_{long}$: stretch it lengthwise and it thins sideways.

Plane stress (isotropic, linear elastic, $\sigma_z=0$)

$$\varepsilon_x=\frac{\sigma_x-\nu\sigma_y}{E},\ \ \varepsilon_y=\frac{\sigma_y-\nu\sigma_x}{E},\ \ \gamma_{xy}=\frac{\tau_{xy}}{G},\ \ G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}$$ $$\sigma_x=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}(\varepsilon_x+\nu\varepsilon_y),\ \ \sigma_y=\frac{E}{1-\nu^2}(\varepsilon_y+\nu\varepsilon_x),\ \ \tau_{xy}=G\gamma_{xy}$$ ⚠ These are plane stress equations ($\sigma_z=0$, $\varepsilon_z\ne0$). Plane strain ($\varepsilon_z=0$) uses different ones.

Worked: stress from strain

$E=200$ GPa $=200000$ MPa, $\nu=0.3$, $\varepsilon_x=500\mu$, $\varepsilon_y=200\mu$, $\gamma=300\mu$.
$E/(1-\nu^2)=200000/0.91=219\,780$ MPa; $G=200000/2.6=76\,923$ MPa.
$\sigma_x=219780(500+0.3\cdot200)\times10^{-6}=\mathbf{123.1}$ MPa
$\sigma_y=219780(200+0.3\cdot500)\times10^{-6}=\mathbf{76.9}$ MPa
$\tau_{xy}=76923\cdot300\times10^{-6}=\mathbf{23.1}$ MPa

Worked: strain from stress

$\sigma_x=100,\sigma_y=50,\tau=30$ MPa.
$\varepsilon_x=(100-0.3\cdot50)/200000=\mathbf{425\mu}$; $\varepsilon_y=(50-0.3\cdot100)/200000=\mathbf{100\mu}$; $\gamma=30/76923=\mathbf{390\mu}$.
Stress→strain divides by E (direct); strain→stress needs the $1/(1-\nu^2)$ factor because both strains affect each stress.
